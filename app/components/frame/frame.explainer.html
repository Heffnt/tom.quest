The frame

The frame is the layout every jarvis page of tom.Quest renders inside. A jarvis page is one of the pages that show the Jarvis agent system's own record: today that is /tts, the todo page, and later /agents and /observe. Every other page of the site keeps its own layout and the navigation bar across its top. This page explains what the frame shows, where each part sits, how it opens and closes, and why it is built this way. It describes the code in app/components/frame/, the directory this file sits in.

What the screen is made of

The screen is divided into a center and up to four drawers, one for each edge: top, bottom, left and right. The center is the area in the middle of the screen where the page shows its main content. A drawer is a panel that slides in from its edge and lies over the center until it is put away. Every drawer has a handle: a thin strip that is the drawer's outer edge. While the drawer is shut, only its handle shows, lying flush against the edge of the screen. When the drawer is pulled out, the handle stays attached to the drawer's inner edge and travels with it toward the center. A handle carries the drawer's label, a few live numbers about what the drawer holds (so a shut drawer still reports its state), and any buttons the page gives it. The picture below places each part where it sits on the screen. It draws the drawers beside the center so that every label can be read; on the real screen a drawer that is pulled out lies over the edge of the center instead.

All four handles are the same thickness, 28 pixels; while two looks are being compared, the second look makes all four 44 pixels. The center is inset by one handle's thickness on every side, so at a screen of 1440 by 900 pixels it is 1384 by 844 pixels, and nothing a drawer does changes that.

The corners are shared out in a fixed order: the top first, then the sides, then the bottom, the way VS Code lays out its title bar, side bars and panel. The top handle and the top drawer span the full width of the screen. The left and right handles and drawers run from just below the top handle down to the bottom of the screen. The bottom handle and drawer sit between the two side handles. So the top edge is one unbroken strip, the sides have the full height, and the bottom is the part that gives way.

The top handle also carries the parts that belong to the site rather than to the page: the site's symbol in the top-left corner, which returns to the home page; the page's name followed by its (i) button; one line of the page's state with counts; then, after a thin divider, the top drawer's own label and numbers; and at the right end a field to navigate to another page, the account button (which reads "Log in" for a visitor and shows the username once signed in) and, for the site's owner only, a dot for the connection to the database. These are buttons on the handle like any other, and they travel with it when the top drawer is pulled out.

What each drawer holds

Each drawer has one role on every page, so the same edge means the same kind of content wherever it is opened. The roles run from the most general content to the most specific: the top chooses what the page covers, the left lists everything, the right shows one thing in full, and the bottom shows the raw rows underneath. The frame itself knows nothing about what a drawer holds; the roles are a rule for the pages, and every drawer opens, drags and shuts the same way whatever is inside.

A page may set, for each drawer, the size it first opens at and the smallest and largest size it may be dragged to; without them a drawer first opens at 320 pixels and may be dragged between 160 pixels and 85% of the screen. Whatever the page sets, a drawer never grows so far that its handle would meet the opposite one. A page may also leave an edge out, and then that edge has no handle and the center reaches the screen's edge there.

On /tts the bottom drawer holds the event stream. An event is one row of the dtsEvents table in the site's Convex database: a record, written at the moment something happened, of a todo being captured, a ruling being made or applied, a delegate answering, a session ending, a status changing, and every other kind the system records. The drawer reads the newest 200 rows through the query api.tts.listRecentEvents, newest first, and shows each one's time, its kind and the statement of the todo it concerns. Pressing a row shows the whole row as JSON beneath it, and pressing it again hides it. The rows update live: Convex pushes a new row to the page as soon as it is written. For the signed-in owner of the site the drawer also holds a diagnostics column: the current route, the signed-in role, the state of the connection to Convex, the screen size, the last captured console warnings and errors, and a button that copies all of it as text.

What a handle shows

A handle shows the drawer's label and its signals, and may hold buttons. A signal is one typed value the page computes from the same data its drawer shows, so the handle and the drawer cannot disagree. There are three kinds, and the page cannot put free markup on a handle: only a label, signals, and buttons that each have a name and an action.

The tones are ok (green), warn (yellow), error (red), accent (orange) and faint (grey), the site's existing colours. When a drawer is shut and its signals differ from what they were at the moment it was last shut, its handle shows a short orange mark before the label, so a change is visible without opening the drawer. The handle of an open drawer has an orange edge. On a top or bottom handle the label reads across; on a side handle it runs along the handle, the left one reading from bottom to top and the right one from top to bottom (in the second look it stands upright instead, stacked a few words to a line).

Pulling a drawer out and putting it away

The whole handle is the control, not a button on it. Press it anywhere along its length and the drawer slides out to the size it was last left at, or to the page's first size the first time. Press the handle of an open drawer, which now sits near the center, and the drawer slides away. Press and drag the handle instead and the drawer follows the pointer to any size: the handle stays under the pointer for as long as the button or finger is down, the drawer stops at its largest size, and letting go keeps the size. A drag of fewer than 4 pixels counts as a press. Let go of a drawer dragged to less than half its smallest size and it slides shut; let go of one between that and its smallest size and it settles at its smallest. Pulled out less than its smallest size, a drawer slides rather than squeezes its content; beyond that it grows.

A button on a handle, such as the (i) or the account button on the top handle, only does its own thing: pressing it never opens, shuts or drags the drawer. The drawer slides open or shut in 150 milliseconds, and not at all when the reader's system asks for reduced motion; while it is being dragged it moves with the pointer and never animates. The same drag works with a finger on a phone. It starts on the handle, which lies inside the page, so it does not compete with the phone's own gestures from the very edge of the screen: a swipe from the left or right edge goes back in the browser's history on iOS and is the system back gesture on Android, the bottom edge opens the home screen, and the top edge opens the notifications.

Which drawers are open, the size each was last left at, and which side drawer was opened last are remembered per page in the browser's local storage, through a Zustand store (frame-store.ts), so a page reopens as it was left. None of it is written into the page's address.

How the drawers overlap

A drawer always lies over the center and never pushes it aside: the center's size and position are fixed by the handles' thickness alone, so pulling, dragging or putting away a drawer moves nothing in the center by a single pixel. Where two open drawers meet, they stack the way the corners are shared out: the top drawer lies over the sides, and the sides lie over the bottom's ends. Of the two sides, the one opened last lies over the other. A drawer's handle is part of the drawer and shares its layer, so an open drawer can cover part of another drawer's handle, never all of it. The layers are the --z-* values at the top of app/globals.css, the one list every stacked surface of the site takes its layer from.

There is no darkened layer behind a drawer, and a drawer never closes by itself or because another opened: it stays out until its handle or the Escape key puts it away. The same holds on a phone, where the handles are the same as on a desktop screen and a drawer is never wider than leaves room for the opposite handle.

The (i) button and explainers

An (i) button sits right after the name of the thing it explains, one per component and never one per control. Pressing it opens that component's explainer across the whole screen, with a header strip holding the explainer's title and a × button; the × or the Escape key closes it. An explainer is one self-contained HTML file committed beside the code it describes and named <name>.explainer.html; this page is frame.explainer.html, and it opens from the (i) after the page's name.

The explainer is shown in an iframe, an embedded browser page, with two protections. The iframe is sandboxed with scripts allowed but without its own origin, so the script that draws its diagram runs as a page from nowhere: it cannot read the site's storage, cookies or page, and it cannot navigate the site, submit a form or open a window. And the viewer inserts a Content-Security-Policy tag at the start of the explainer's head that refuses every network request, so an explainer that tried to load anything would fail instead. An explainer carries a light and a dark palette and chooses between them by the reader's system setting, and inside an iframe that setting is the operating system's, not the site's. The site is always dark, so before showing the file the viewer rewrites its palette choice so that the dark palette always applies; the same file opened from disk still follows the system setting.

The explainer argument of the (i) is typed: scripts/gen-explainers.mjs finds every *.explainer.html under app/ and writes explainer-registry.generated.ts, whose type ExplainerId lists exactly the names that have a file, so naming an explainer that does not exist fails the type check. Each file becomes its own module that is loaded only when its (i) is pressed, so no page downloads explainers it does not open. explainers.test.ts fails when the committed registry differs from what the script would write.

What the frame changes and what it leaves alone

A page is marked as a frame page by frame: true on its row in app/components/page-routes.ts. For such a page, app/components/app-shell.tsx renders no navigation bar and no top padding, and the page renders <Frame> itself, passing its name, its center and, for each drawer, its handle (a label, signals and buttons), its content and, if it wants, the size it first opens at and its bounds. The page passes content only: the frame owns every position, layer and motion. The diagnostics panel that on other pages slides in from the left and pushes the page to the right is not drawn on a frame page, because pushing the center is what the frame rules out; its contents are the diagnostics column of the bottom drawer instead. Every page not marked frame: true is unchanged: it keeps the navigation bar, its own layout and the side diagnostics panel. Inside the frame all text is set in IBM Plex Sans, with IBM Plex Mono for numbers, identifiers and code; the site's display face is not used there.
